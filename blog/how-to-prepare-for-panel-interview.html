How to Prepare for a Panel Interview

Published August 2026 · RoleCall Interview Prep

You walk into the room and there are four people sitting across the table instead of one. Your stomach drops. Panel interviews are one of the most intimidating formats in the hiring process, but they are also one of the most common — especially in healthcare, education, government, and corporate roles.

The good news is that panel interviews follow predictable patterns. Once you understand the format and prepare strategically, you can turn the extra faces into an advantage rather than a source of anxiety.

What Is a Panel Interview?

A panel interview is when two or more interviewers meet with you at the same time. Typically, the panel includes a mix of people who will interact with you in the role: your potential direct manager, a peer from the team, someone from HR, and occasionally a cross-functional stakeholder.

Each panelist is evaluating you from a different angle. The hiring manager cares about your skills and fit. HR is assessing culture alignment and compliance. The team member wants to know if you will be easy to work with. Understanding this helps you tailor your answers to address multiple perspectives at once.

Why Companies Use Panel Interviews

Panel interviews are not designed to make you uncomfortable. Companies use them for practical reasons:

  • Efficiency: One 45-minute panel replaces three separate 30-minute interviews, which speeds up the hiring process for everyone.
  • Multiple perspectives: Different interviewers catch different things. One person might focus on technical skills while another evaluates communication style.
  • Reduced bias: A group decision is less likely to be swayed by one person's personal preferences or blind spots.
  • Team fit assessment: The panel gets to see how you interact with multiple personality types, which mirrors how you will actually work.

Before the Interview: Preparation Checklist

Preparation for a panel interview is similar to a one-on-one, but with a few extra steps:

Research each panelist. Ask the recruiter or scheduling contact who will be on the panel. Look up each person on LinkedIn. Note their role, how long they have been at the company, and any shared connections or interests. This gives you conversation anchors and helps you direct answers to the right person.

Prepare stories that work for multiple evaluators. A well-chosen example can demonstrate technical skill to the hiring manager, teamwork to the peer, and communication ability to HR all at the same time. Use the STAR method to keep your stories structured and concise.

Practice with multiple people. Ask two or three friends or family members to sit across from you and fire questions. Get used to making eye contact with different people while speaking. This is the single most effective way to reduce panel interview anxiety.

Bring enough copies of your resume. Print one copy for each panelist plus one for yourself. Even if they have digital copies, having a physical resume shows preparation and gives them something to reference while you speak.

During the Interview: How to Connect with Everyone

Address the person who asked the question, then include the group. Start your answer by making eye contact with the panelist who posed the question. As you develop your answer, naturally shift your gaze to include the others. This keeps everyone engaged without feeling like you are ignoring the person who asked.

Use names. When you can naturally work a panelist's name into your response, it builds rapport. "That's a great question, Sarah. In my experience..." It also helps you remember who is who if the panel is large.

Tailor answers to the room. If the HR manager asks about a challenge you faced, emphasize the interpersonal and process aspects. If the technical lead asks the same question, lean into the technical details. Read the room and adjust.

Take your time. Panel interviews can feel rushed because you are managing multiple people's attention. Do not let that pressure you into giving short, surface-level answers. A thoughtful 90-second answer beats three rushed 20-second ones.

Manage the quiet panelist. There is often one person who says very little. Make a point to include them with eye contact and, if appropriate, direct a question to them at the end: "Is there anything about my background you would like me to elaborate on?"

Common Panel Interview Questions

Panel interviews tend to include a mix of behavioral, situational, and role-specific questions. Here are the ones that come up most often:

  • "Tell us about yourself." (Use the Present → Past → Future framework.)
  • "Describe a time you had to work with a difficult colleague."
  • "How do you prioritize competing deadlines?"
  • "Give an example of when you had to learn something quickly."
  • "Why are you interested in this role?" (See our guide on answering 'Why do you want to work here?')
  • "Where do you see yourself in five years?" (See our guide on answering this question.)
  • "Do you have any questions for us?"

Pro tip: Prepare at least five questions to ask the panel. In a one-on-one, two or three questions is enough. In a panel, you want enough to direct at least one question to each panelist or their area of expertise.

Questions to Ask a Panel

Asking thoughtful questions shows you are serious about the role and helps you evaluate whether the team is right for you:

  • To the hiring manager: "What does success look like in this role after the first 90 days?"
  • To the team member: "What is the team dynamic like? How does the team typically collaborate?"
  • To HR: "What does the onboarding process look like for this role?"
  • To the cross-functional stakeholder: "How does this team interact with other departments?"
  • To the whole panel: "What is the biggest challenge the team is facing right now that this role could help solve?"

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Only looking at one panelist. If you stare at the hiring manager the entire time, the other panelists will feel excluded and may disengage.
  • Giving the same answer to every question. Panelists compare notes afterward. If every answer sounds the same, it signals a lack of depth.
  • Forgetting names. Write them down when you sit down. A small notepad on the table is perfectly acceptable.
  • Being thrown off by follow-up questions. Panelists will sometimes build on each other's questions. This is normal — it means they are engaged, not trying to trip you up.
  • Not sending individual thank-you emails. Send a personalized thank-you to each panelist within 24 hours. Reference something specific from your conversation with each person.

After the Interview

Panel interviews often take longer to get feedback on because multiple people need to debrief and align. If the recruiter said you would hear back in a week, wait the full week before following up. When you do follow up, send one email to the recruiter or hiring manager — do not email every panelist separately unless you have a specific reason to.

Final Thoughts

Panel interviews feel intimidating because you are managing multiple people's attention at once. But the reality is that they give you more opportunities to connect, more chances to showcase different strengths, and a clearer picture of who you will be working with.

Prepare your stories, research the panelists, practice with multiple people, and remember: every person at that table wants you to be the right candidate. They are not there to grill you — they are there to find their next teammate.
